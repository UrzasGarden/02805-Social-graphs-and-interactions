The Forger: can word counting catch an AI writing Wikipedia?

We asked Claude Fable 5.1, a frontier language model, to forge the Wikipedia articles of 60 Marvel characters. Can a detector built only from this week's tools (counts, hapaxes, n-grams and bags of words) tell the forgeries from the real articles? And does it beat you? Play the game below and find out.

What we did

  1. Real side. 60 characters from the course's 303-article Marvel network, 20 each from the high, middle and low in-degree tiers. Every page went through one cleaning function, and we kept its first ≤1500 words.
  2. Forged side. For each character a separate Fable 5.1 AI was told only the name, the one-line description, the characters the real article links to, and the target length. It never saw the real article. We checked every forger's log: it received exactly our prompt and made no other tool call than writing its article. The forgeries then went through the same cleaning as the real pages.
  3. Detector. Twelve counts per document: sentence length, punctuation rates, stopword share, type/token and hapax ratios on 400-word windows, the share of word pairs never seen in the other real articles, and bag-of-words similarity to all articles and to the character's network neighbours. A logistic regression on these, tested so that it never sees the character it is judging (grouped cross-validation).

The plan was an arms race: tell the forger what gave it away and let it try again. We stopped after round 1, so everything here is the forger's first attempt.

What surprised us

  • The forgeries are more typical than the real thing. We expected the AI to give itself away with odd word pairs. It does the opposite. Only 28% of a forgery's word pairs are missing from the other real articles, against 32% for real pages. Its vocabulary is also closer to the average Marvel article. Fable writes the average article; real editors write stranger ones.
  • The tell is rhythm, not vocabulary. Commas, stopwords and semicolons barely separate the two. Long sentences, extra parentheses and dashes do (forgeries use about three times as many dashes).
  • Famous characters look more "forged", real or not. The detector's average forgery score for real pages of high in-degree characters is 0.32, against 0.15 for the least-linked ones. Popular articles have the long, parenthesis-heavy sentences the forger writes.
  • The only two pairs the detector lost are the two broken prompts. For Scarlet Spider and Kristoff Vernard, the course data's one-line description is cut off mid-sentence. With two cases this may be chance, but it is the kind of thing we would test next.

Did the forger copy Wikipedia? Mostly the template.

The longest run of words a forgery shares with its own real article is 33 tokens, for Shuri AI:

appearing in american comic books published by marvel comics created by writer reginald hudlin and artist john romita jr the character first appeared in black panther vol 4 2 may 2005 shuri is

That is Wikipedia's stock opening sentence filled in with facts, not memorised prose. Real pages share runs this long with each other too. The median longest run is 15 tokens for a forgery against its own article, and also 15 for a real page against the other real pages.

A real article the detector calls a forgery

The real Black Widow article gets a 93% forgery score. Its opening packs a Russian transliteration and several issue references into parentheses (1.5 per 100 words) and runs long sentences. On counts alone it looks like Fable. The detector still gets the pair right, because Fable's Black Widow AI is even more extreme: 30 words per sentence and 1.7 parentheses per 100.

Is "sentence length" just a formatting artefact?

Real Wikipedia pages sometimes contain flattened lists, which would make sentences look short. We re-measured using only proper prose paragraphs: real 20.2 against forged 24.5 words per sentence, the same gap. The forger really does write longer sentences.

One limitation

You and the detector are not playing the same game. You read a 120-word opening. The detector scores the whole document, up to 1500 words, where sentence length and punctuation have room to add up. A fair human-vs-machine comparison would give the detector the same 120 words. Expect its accuracy to drop there, because these statistics are noisy on short texts.
