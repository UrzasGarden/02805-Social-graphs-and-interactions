Spider-Man is a superhero appearing in American comic books published by Marvel Comics. Created by writer-editor Stan Lee and artist Steve Ditko, he first appeared in the anthology comic book Amazing Fantasy #15 (August 1962) during the Silver Age of Comic Books. The character has since been featured in comic books, television series, films, video games and other media. Spider-Man's alter ego is Peter Parker, an orphaned teenager raised by his Aunt May and Uncle Ben in Forest Hills, Queens, New York City. After being bitten by a radioactive spider, Parker gains superhuman strength, speed and agility, the ability to cling to most surfaces, and a precognitive "spider-sense" that warns him of danger. He builds wrist-mounted devices that fire artificial webbing of his own design.

Lee and Ditko conceived the character as an adolescent whose personal problems, including money trouble, romantic disappointments and the competing demands of school and work, were given as much weight as his costumed adventures. This approach departed from the convention of the period, in which teenage characters were usually sidekicks to adult heroes. Spider-Man has been a member of the Avengers and the Fantastic Four, and his supporting cast and rogues' gallery, including Mary Jane Watson, Gwen Stacy, Harry Osborn, J. Jonah Jameson, the Green Goblin, Doctor Octopus and Venom, are among the best known in the medium.

Spider-Man is Marvel's flagship character and corporate mascot and is frequently cited as one of the most popular superheroes ever created. He has appeared in several animated and live-action television series, a long-running newspaper strip and a series of feature films, having been played on screen by Tobey Maguire, Andrew Garfield and Tom Holland. Other characters have taken up the Spider-Man identity, among them Miles Morales and Ben Reilly, and the character has inspired a family of spider-themed heroes including Spider-Woman (Gwen Stacy), Silk and Spider-Man Noir.

== Publication history ==

== Creation and development ==

In 1962, with the Fantastic Four and the Hulk selling well, Marvel editor Stan Lee was seeking a new character. In his later accounts, Lee said he wanted a teenage hero who was not a sidekick and who was burdened by ordinary troubles, and that the idea of a hero with the abilities of a spider occurred to him while watching an insect climb a wall. Publisher Martin Goodman was reportedly reluctant, objecting that readers disliked spiders, that a teenager could not carry a title and that a hero with problems was not heroic. Lee was nonetheless permitted to use the character in Amazing Fantasy, an anthology that was about to be cancelled, since little would be lost if the story failed.

Lee first assigned the feature to Jack Kirby. According to Lee, Kirby's pages depicted a muscular, conventionally heroic figure, which was not what Lee had in mind, and he turned the assignment over to Steve Ditko. Ditko designed the costume, including the full face mask and the web pattern, and devised the web-shooters and the character's distinctive poses and movement. Kirby drew the cover of Amazing Fantasy #15 after Lee rejected Ditko's version. The extent of Kirby's contribution to the concept has been disputed; Kirby stated that he had brought Lee an idea for a character called Spiderman derived from an earlier project, the Silver Spider, which he had developed with Joe Simon. Simon later said that the character had originated with him under the name the Fly.

The relative contributions of Lee and Ditko have also been debated. Ditko maintained that he plotted the stories, designed the villains and determined much of the character's world once the two adopted the "Marvel Method", in which the artist worked from a brief synopsis and the writer added dialogue afterward. Beginning with The Amazing Spider-Man #25 (June 1965), Ditko received a plotting credit. Lee acknowledged Ditko as co-creator but continued to describe the character's conception as his own.

== 1960s and 1970s ==

Sales of Amazing Fantasy #15 were strong enough that Goodman authorized a solo title, and The Amazing Spider-Man #1 was cover-dated March 1963. The series introduced much of the supporting cast and most of the character's principal enemies within its first years, including J. Jonah Jameson, the Chameleon, the Vulture, Doctor Octopus, the Sandman, the Lizard, Electro, Mysterio, the Green Goblin and Kraven the Hunter. Ditko left Marvel after The Amazing Spider-Man #38 (July 1966) following a breakdown in his working relationship with Lee; the two had reportedly stopped speaking some time before his departure. John Romita Sr. succeeded him, gave Parker a more conventionally attractive appearance, introduced Mary Jane Watson in full in issue #42 (November 1966) and oversaw the title's rise to Marvel's best-selling comic.

A second ongoing title, The Spectacular Spider-Man, was launched in 1968 as a black-and-white magazine but lasted only two issues; the name was revived in 1976 for a color comic book that ran until 1998. In The Amazing Spider-Man #96–98 (May–July 1971), Lee wrote a story about drug abuse at the request of the United States Department of Health, Education, and Welfare. The Comics Code Authority refused to approve the issues, and Marvel published them without the Code seal, which contributed to a revision of the Code later that year. Gerry Conway took over as writer in 1972 and, with artist Gil Kane, killed Gwen Stacy in "The Night Gwen Stacy Died" in The Amazing Spider-Man #121–122 (June–July 1973), an event often cited as marking the end of the Silver Age. Conway and Ross Andru introduced the Punisher in issue #129 (February 1974). A newspaper strip written by Lee and drawn by Romita began in January 1977 and ran until 2019. Marv Wolfman and Keith Pollard introduced the Black Cat in The Amazing Spider-Man #194 (July 1979).

== 1980s and 1990s ==

Roger Stern's run in the early 1980s produced the Hobgoblin and the well-regarded single-issue story "The Kid Who Collects Spider-Man" in issue #248 (January 1984). In the 1984 crossover Secret Wars, Spider-Man acquired a black costume that was later revealed to be an alien symbiote; after Parker rejected it, the symbiote bonded with Eddie Brock to become Venom, who first appeared in full in The Amazing Spider-Man #300 (May 1988), written by David Michelinie and drawn by Todd McFarlane. Peter Parker married Mary Jane Watson in The Amazing Spider-Man Annual #21 (1987), an event coordinated with the newspaper strip. Michelinie and McFarlane's run was a commercial success, and in 1990 McFarlane was given a new adjectiveless Spider-Man title whose first issue sold more than 2.5 million copies, then a record for the industry.

In 1994 Marvel began the "Clone Saga", a lengthy storyline running through all of the Spider-Man titles that reintroduced Ben Reilly, a clone of Parker from a 1975 story, as the Scarlet Spider. The storyline revealed, and later retracted, that Reilly was the original and Parker the clone, and it was widely criticized for its length and for the editorial reversals that prolonged it. After its conclusion in 1996, Marvel relaunched the line in 1999 under writer Howard Mackie and artist John Byrne, restarting The Amazing Spider-Man with a new issue #1. Mayday Parker, the daughter of Peter and Mary Jane in a possible future, debuted in What If #105 (February 1998) and received her own series, Spider-Girl, later that year.

== 2000s and 2010s ==

J. Michael Straczynski wrote The Amazing Spider-Man from 2001 to 2007, introducing a totemic explanation for the character's powers and having Spider-Man reveal his identity to the public during the 2006–2007 crossover "Civil War". The 2007 storyline "One More Day", by Straczynski and editor-in-chief Joe Quesada, removed the marriage from continuity, after which the title was published three times a month under the "Brand New Day" banner with a rotating team of writers. Dan Slott became the sole writer in 2010 and remained for most of the decade, writing "Spider-Island", the 2013–2014 period in which Doctor Octopus occupied Parker's body in The Superior Spider-Man, and the 2014–2015 crossover "Spider-Verse", which gathered Spider-Man characters from across Marvel's alternate realities, among them Spider-Man Noir, Spider-UK, Spider-Woman (Gwen Stacy) and Silk, a new character bitten by the same spider as Parker. Nick Spencer followed Slott in 2018, and Zeb Wells took over the series in 2022.
